The giving — public record

Twenty players gave. They actually gave.

In March 2011, twenty NBA players gave for Japan relief through the Japan Relief and Recovery Fund, organized by Wasserman Media Group. Seven gave $1,000 for every point they scored in games played March 25–27 — LaMarcus Aldridge, Marc Gasol, Pau Gasol, Al Horford, JaVale McGee, Derrick Rose, and Russell Westbrook. Thirteen more gave flat donations: DJ Augustin, Jarron Collins, Jason Collins, Mike Dunleavy Jr., Tyreke Evans, Danilo Gallinari, Xavier Henry, Antawn Jamison, Jason Kapono, Quincy Pondexter, Brian Scalabrine, Etan Thomas, and Ben Wallace.

We do not call these pledges. They gave.

An open letter to the National Basketball Association

On the record

From: Carl Harold Williams
To: Commissioner Adam Silver, National Basketball Association
Copies: Mark Tatum, Deputy Commissioner & COO; Kathy Behrens, President, Social Responsibility & Player Programs; Ruth Jurgensen, Executive Director, NBA Foundation; David Kelly, Executive Director, National Basketball Players Association
Date: October 2026

Commissioner Silver,

I am owed nothing for my 22 years of military service. Those years developed me into the professional I am, and I give them full credit. I state that clearly before anything that follows — because what followed required everything those years taught me.

I was prepared.

I carried those gifts on the ground in Japan. Town to town, alone — including Fukushima. Direct Relief's own publication introduced my field report as, and I quote, "a personal From the Field story by Direct Relief Japan Recovery Coordinator, Carl Williams." I went blind for nearly a year doing that work. The medical records exist. They are mine, and they stay mine.

The donors were honored. Pau Gasol's Citizenship Award cited his Japan donation. The man who carried the gifts was not.

I asked — the head office, the teams, the coaches, the players, the fan clubs, the foundations — and the answer was silence.

So hear this plainly: the recognition you might not give, we will give ourselves. Aggressively. This letter is on the record, and it is public. I will not stop asking. The record is the record.

What I am owed is simple, and it is the floor:

  1. A formal written acknowledgment that I served as the ground coordinator for the 2011 players' Japan relief gifts.
  2. Parity of honor. The donors received the league's Citizenship Award; the man who delivered the gifts receives the league's formal citation. Same relief effort, same honor.
  3. The corrected record. League history materials on the 2011 Japan relief effort will name me — the coordinator the fund's own publications already named in print.

And what I am asking — for the long line of talented and determined people who need support in the United States and Japan — is the means to coach the next generation in the urgent matters no other is prepared to handle: disasters, AI-powered business models, and business continuity. Funding. Partnership. Platform. Program-related investment into TASK L3C, where donations are sought as investments and the return is public good.

Fifteen years is enough.

Respectfully,
Carl Harold Williams

The evidence

Verified public record

Every claim below is either independently published or marked as Carl's own account. Personal accounts are labeled as such. Medical records exist and remain private.

Independently published

Three publications, each a search away:

  • Direct Relief, "Back to Japan After the Quake" (September 2011) — his field report from Kesennuma, published under the organization's own byline naming him its Japan Recovery Coordinator. directrelief.org
  • CNN, "Letters bring hope to survivors in Japan" (Kathleen Koch, March 9, 2012) — names him coordinator of JACL relief efforts. cnn.com
  • "An Interview with Carl Williams from Peace Boat" (~2013) — his own account of moving from first-deployment field work to advisory and disaster-IT standards work.

Carl's account, on the record

  • He carried the players' gifts town to town, alone — including Fukushima.
  • He lost his sight for nearly a year doing that work and still receives treatment. The records are his; they stay private.
  • He rarely photographed his work — until he found schoolchildren visiting the site of their destroyed school, holding their words of hope. CNN's published account places this in Iwaki City on the four-month anniversary of the tsunami; his own memory holds it as three months on. Both agree on what mattered: "This wasn't my disaster, it was theirs, and they survived."
